It's a familiar scene. In January, leadership, product, engineering and commercial teams sit down and agree a roadmap for the year. It's ambitious, well presented and everyone signs off. People leave the meeting feeling aligned.

By June, a third of it has been quietly dropped, another third is late, and the team is busy with things that were never on it. When someone asks what happened, nobody can quite say. There wasn't one big failure. The roadmap just stopped being true, a little at a time.

I've seen this enough times to believe it's not bad luck. Most of these roadmaps were in trouble from the meeting where everyone agreed to them.

## Agreement is cheap

A roadmap meeting usually optimises for consensus. Each stakeholder wants their priorities on the list, and the easiest way to get everyone to sign off is to include something for everyone.

The result is a roadmap that everyone agrees with because it doesn't force anyone to give anything up. That's not alignment. It's a list of wishes with dates attached.

Real alignment shows up in what's left out. If your roadmap meeting didn't produce at least one disappointed stakeholder, it probably didn't make any real choices.

## Why these roadmaps fail

### It was never sized against capacity

Roadmap items are often estimated at a very high level, if at all, and nobody adds them up against the team's realistic capacity. When engineering raises concerns, the answer is often "we'll figure it out" or "let's see how far we get". That's how a nine-month plan ends up on a six-month team.

A rough check helps. Take the team's actual throughput over the last two quarters, subtract the share that went on unplanned work, and compare it with what the roadmap needs. If the gap is large, the roadmap is fiction.

### It ignored the work that was already there

Maintenance, technical debt, compliance changes, platform upgrades and operational requests don't stop because a new roadmap was agreed. In many teams they consume 30 to 50% of capacity. A roadmap that assumes 100% of capacity goes to new features is broken before it starts.

### Items were outputs, not outcomes

"Launch a loyalty programme" is an output. When it runs into trouble, the only options are to cut it or push the date. "Increase 90-day retention for new depositors" is an outcome. When the planned solution runs into trouble, the team can look for another way to get there.

Outcome-based roadmaps are more resilient because they give the team room to adapt without abandoning the goal.

### Dependencies weren't mapped

Many roadmap items depend on other teams, third-party vendors or regulatory approvals. If these aren't identified upfront, they surface mid-quarter as blockers. Each one costs a few weeks, and a few weeks at a time is how a roadmap slips by six months.

### Nobody owned the trade-offs

When new priorities arrived, and they always do, they were added without anything being removed. There was no forum or owner to say: "If this goes in, what comes out?"

### It was never revisited

The roadmap was treated as a commitment made once, not a plan to be managed. By the time anyone looked at it seriously again, it was too far from reality to recover.

## Building a roadmap that survives

Here's what I do differently.

**Start from capacity.** Before discussing features, agree how much capacity is realistically available for new work after maintenance and unplanned demand. Plan to that number, not to the total.

**Frame items as outcomes with candidate solutions.** Each item has a goal, a metric and a first idea of how to get there. The idea can change. The goal shouldn't, at least not without a conversation.

**Force a ranking.** Not tiers, not "must have" for half the list. A strict order. When capacity runs out, the line falls where it falls. Items below the line are explicitly not happening this quarter.

**Map dependencies before committing.** For each item, list what it needs from outside the team and when. Anything with a long lead time gets started early or flagged as a risk.

**Plan in horizons.** Commit firmly to the next quarter. Hold the following quarter as a likely direction. Treat anything beyond that as intent, not commitment.

**Review it monthly.** A short session with the same people who agreed it. What's on track, what's at risk, what's changed, and what needs to come out to make room for something new.

**Make swaps explicit.** When a new priority arrives, the person requesting it joins the conversation about what it replaces. This one rule changes behaviour more than any tool.

## Saying no in January

All of this comes down to having the uncomfortable conversation early. A roadmap that disappoints someone in January but delivers in June is far better than one that pleases everyone in January and disappoints everyone by summer.

If your last roadmap ended up as a document nobody looks at, don't start the next one by asking what everyone wants. Start by asking how much you can actually do, and then decide together what matters most within that limit. It's a harder meeting. It's also the only kind of roadmap meeting that's worth having.
