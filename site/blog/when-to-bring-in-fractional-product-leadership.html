When to Bring In Fractional Product Leadership

23 Jun 2026 3 min read

A full-time head of product is a big hire to get wrong. For many companies, a few days a week of senior product judgment does the job better. The signs you need it, and what to expect from it.

Most companies reach a point where product decisions outgrow the people making them. The founder can’t be in every prioritisation call any more. Engineering is shipping, but nobody can say clearly why those features and not others. Different parts of the business pull the roadmap in different directions.

The usual answer is to hire a head of product. That’s a good answer, eventually. It’s also a slow, expensive hire, and a costly one to get wrong at a stage when the company might not yet know exactly what it needs from the role.

Fractional product leadership sits in between: an experienced product lead working a set number of days a week, embedded in the team, accountable for outcomes.

Signs you need it

These are the situations where we see it make the biggest difference.

The roadmap is a list of requests. Features come from whoever asked loudest or most recently, and nobody can connect them to a business goal.

Engineering is busy, but results aren’t moving. Releases go out, velocity looks fine, and revenue, retention or conversion stays flat.

The founder or CTO is the product owner by default, and it’s eating their week. Strategic work is slipping because they’re writing tickets and refereeing priorities.

You run several brands or markets on one platform, and they’re drifting apart. Each one has its own backlog, features exist on some and not others, and nobody has a view across all of them.

You’re about to make a big product bet, like a new market, a platform rebuild or a major integration, and nobody in the room has done it before.

You lost a product lead and need the work covered properly while you hire, rather than spread across people who already have full jobs.

What it is, and what it isn’t

Fractional leadership is hands-on. It means owning the roadmap, running prioritisation, working directly with engineering and design, talking to the commercial side, and being accountable for whether the work moves the numbers.

It isn’t a strategy deck delivered at the end of six weeks. It isn’t a consultant who interviews everyone and leaves a report. And it isn’t a project manager chasing tickets.

A good fractional lead also doesn’t micromanage. The team keeps running its own day-to-day work. What changes is that someone with senior judgment sets direction, makes the trade-offs, expects clear reporting, and steps in when something is actually going wrong.

What the first 90 days usually look like

The first month is mostly understanding. How the product really works today, what the data says, where the team’s time actually goes, and which problems everyone knows about but nobody owns.

The second month is direction. A short list of priorities tied to business outcomes, a roadmap the team can deliver, and a clear process for how new requests get decided.

The third month is delivery. Work shipping against the new priorities, measurement in place to see whether it’s working, and the first honest review of what changed.

By the end, the company should have a product function that runs, a clearer view of what a permanent head of product would need to do, and in many cases the confidence to make that hire well.

Questions to ask before you engage someone

Have they owned a roadmap under real pressure, with revenue and deadlines attached? Have they worked in an environment with the kind of complexity you have: several brands, several markets, regulation, heavy third-party integration? Will they work inside your tools and your team, or alongside them? How will they report progress, and against which outcomes?

And the most useful one: what would they stop doing in your organisation in the first month? A good answer tells you they’ll make decisions, not just add process.

The trade-off

Fractional leadership gives you senior judgment without a permanent senior salary, and you can scale it up or down as the business changes. The trade-off is availability. Someone working two or three days a week won’t be in every meeting, so the team needs to be capable of running without them in between.

For most growing product organisations, that’s a feature. It forces clarity about who decides what, and it leaves the team with structure that holds after the engagement ends.
