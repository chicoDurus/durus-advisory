I'm a certified Scrum product owner. I've introduced Scrum into teams that badly needed it, and I've seen it work well. So take this in the right spirit: a lot of teams running Scrum shouldn't be.

Not because Scrum is bad, but because it's a specific answer to a specific problem. If you don't have that problem, you get the overhead without the benefit.

## What problem Scrum solves

Scrum was designed for teams building a product in conditions of uncertainty. You don't fully know what users need, priorities shift, and you need a regular rhythm to decide what to build next, build it, show it, and learn.

It addresses some very common failures:

- Priorities change daily and the team never finishes anything
- Stakeholders have no idea what's being built until it's released
- Work is "90% done" for weeks because nobody breaks it down
- The team never stops to look at how it's working

If that list sounds like your organisation, Scrum can help a lot. The fixed sprint protects focus. Planning forces prioritisation. The review creates feedback. The retrospective creates improvement.

## When Scrum is the wrong fit

Here are the situations where I've seen Scrum cause more friction than it removes.

**Most of your work is unplanned.** If half of each sprint goes to incidents, urgent operator requests or compliance fixes, your sprint plan is fiction. The team learns that commitments don't mean anything, and planning becomes a ritual. Kanban with a clear intake process usually serves these teams better.

**The team is one or two people.** A daily scrum between two developers who sit next to each other is a conversation, not a meeting. A small team needs a prioritised backlog and a regular check-in with whoever sets priorities. That's it.

**The work is mostly known.** Migrating 40 brands to a new CMS template, or implementing a regulator's published technical standard, involves little discovery. You need a plan, a tracker and a weekly risk review. Two-week sprints with reviews add little.

**Nobody can act as product owner.** Scrum depends on one person who owns the backlog and can make priority calls. If that decision actually sits with a committee, or changes depending on who shouted last, Scrum exposes the problem without fixing it. That can be useful, but only if someone is willing to fix the underlying issue.

**Leadership wants Scrum for predictability alone.** Scrum gives you a rhythm and better visibility. It doesn't make estimates accurate, and velocity is not a productivity metric. If the goal is "tell me exactly when this ships", Scrum will disappoint.

## Five questions to ask first

Before adopting Scrum, or before deciding to keep it, I'd ask these.

1. **Can we hold priorities steady for two weeks?** If not, what would have to change for that to be possible?
2. **Is there one person who owns the backlog and can say no?** If not, Scrum will struggle until there is.
3. **Can the team finish valuable work inside a sprint?** If every piece of work depends on another team, the bottleneck is structural.
4. **What are we hoping to fix?** Name it. "We don't know what's being built" and "we never finish anything" are different problems with different solutions.
5. **Who will protect the process when it's inconvenient?** The first time a senior stakeholder wants something mid-sprint is when Scrum is tested.

If the honest answers are mostly yes, Scrum is probably a good fit. If they're mostly no, start smaller.

## Start with practices, not the whole package

You don't have to adopt a full framework to get most of the value. Some practices pay off almost anywhere:

- **A single, ordered backlog** that everyone can see
- **A visible board** showing what's in progress and what's blocked
- **A limit on work in progress**, so things get finished
- **A regular demo** of what's been built, to the people who asked for it
- **A short retrospective** every few weeks, with one improvement agreed each time

Introduce these, see which ones help, and add structure only where you feel a real gap. If you find yourself needing a fixed cadence and dedicated planning, you'll have grown into Scrum on your own terms. People will understand why each part exists.

## The real question

"Do we need Scrum?" is usually a proxy for a better question: "What's actually stopping us from delivering well?"

Sometimes the answer is lack of focus, and Scrum's sprint boundary helps. Sometimes it's unclear ownership, and no framework fixes that without a leadership decision. Sometimes it's too many dependencies across teams, and that needs a structural answer.

Pick the process that solves the problem you actually have. If that turns out to be Scrum, great. If it isn't, nobody will award you points for running the ceremonies.
