When a product fails, the post-mortem usually blames one of two things. Either the strategy was wrong, or delivery was poor. Wrong market, wrong feature, wrong timing. Or slow engineering, bugs, missed dates.

In my experience, the most common cause is neither. The strategy was reasonable. The team was capable. The product died in between: in the gap where direction is supposed to turn into a plan, and the plan into working software that changes something measurable.

That gap is where I spend most of my time, and it's the reason Durus exists.

## What the gap looks like

It rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a series of small disconnections.

**The goal gets lost in translation.** Leadership wants to improve retention among new players. By the time this reaches the team, it's become a list of features: a welcome journey, a loyalty tier, push notifications. The features get built. Nobody checks whether retention moved.

**Priorities fragment on the way down.** The strategy names three priorities. Each stakeholder interprets them through their own lens and pushes their own requests. The team ends up with fifteen priorities and no way to choose between them.

**Constraints surface too late.** The strategy assumed things about the platform that weren't true. Engineering discovers this mid-build, and the plan has to be reworked under time pressure.

**Feedback doesn't flow back up.** The team learns that a feature isn't working, or that a simpler approach would get the same result. That learning stays at team level. Leadership is still steering by the original plan.

**Nobody owns the whole line.** Leadership owns strategy. Engineering owns delivery. Product is supposed to connect them, but is often too buried in tickets and stakeholder requests to see the full picture.

Each of these is small on its own. Together they explain why so many sensible strategies produce disappointing results.

## Why it's so common

The people who set strategy and the people who deliver it work at different altitudes, on different time horizons, with different information.

Leadership thinks in quarters and years, about markets, revenue and competitors. Delivery teams think in sprints, about tickets, bugs and dependencies. Both views are necessary. But without someone actively translating between them, they drift apart.

In multi-brand, multi-market and regulated environments, the distance is even larger. There are more stakeholders, more constraints and more ways for a clear goal to fragment into conflicting requests.

## Closing the gap

There's no single fix, but a set of practices makes a large difference.

### Keep the outcome attached to the work

Every piece of work on the roadmap should carry the outcome it serves, all the way down to the ticket. When an engineer picks up a task, they should be able to see which goal it contributes to and how success will be measured.

This sounds small. In practice it changes the questions people ask. "Is this the best way to get there?" becomes a natural question, not a challenge to authority.

### Translate strategy into a small number of bets

Don't hand a strategy document to a delivery team. Translate it into three to five concrete bets for the quarter, each with a goal, a metric, a first solution and a named owner. Bets can be wrong. That's the point. They're designed to be tested.

### Test feasibility before committing

Bring engineering into the conversation while the direction is still flexible. A short technical assessment of each major bet will surface the expensive assumptions before they become delivery problems.

### Build a feedback loop that reaches the top

Set up a regular forum, monthly works well, where delivery teams report results against the bets. Not status, but evidence: what moved, what didn't, what was learned. Leadership then adjusts direction based on what's actually happening.

### Measure outcomes, not just delivery

Track whether the bets are working, not only whether the features shipped. A feature that shipped on time and moved nothing is not a success, and treating it as one teaches the organisation the wrong lesson.

### Give someone ownership of the whole line

This is the role that's most often missing. Someone needs to own the connection from strategic goal to shipped outcome: shaping the bets, protecting priorities, raising constraints early, measuring results and reporting back. In smaller organisations it might be a senior product owner. In larger ones it's a head of product or a product operations function.

Without that ownership, everyone does their part well and the whole still fails.

## What it feels like when it works

When the gap is closed, a few things change noticeably.

Roadmap conversations get shorter, because everyone is working from the same small set of goals. Engineers raise concerns earlier, because they understand what matters and feel able to suggest alternatives. Leadership trusts the team more, because they can see results, not just activity. And the team stops shipping features that nobody checks.

Most importantly, good ideas survive. They make it from the strategy deck to the platform intact, or they're changed deliberately based on evidence, not lost along the way.

## The work in the middle

There's a lot of attention on strategy and a lot on delivery. The space between them gets far less, even though it's where so much value is lost.

If your organisation has a clear direction and a capable team, and the results still aren't coming, look at the middle. It's rarely the most visible problem, but it's very often the one that matters most.
