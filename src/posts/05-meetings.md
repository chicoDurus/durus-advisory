When a team complains about meetings, the instinct is to cancel some. Sometimes that's right. More often the problem isn't the number of meetings, it's that nobody knows what each one is supposed to produce.

A meeting with a clear output and a time limit is useful. A meeting without one turns into a status update, a debate or a group therapy session, usually all three.

Here's how I approach the core delivery meetings.

## The one rule for every meeting

Before anything else: every recurring meeting should have a written answer to three questions.

1. **What does this meeting produce?** A decision, a plan, a list of improvements. Something you could point to afterwards.
2. **Who needs to be there to produce it?** Not who might be interested. Who is required.
3. **What has to be true before it starts?** Prepared tickets, a reviewed board, data pulled.

If you can't answer the first question, the meeting probably shouldn't exist.

## Daily standup

**Output:** a shared view of what's blocking the sprint goal, and who's going to unblock it.

**Common failure:** everyone recites what they did yesterday to the scrum master or manager. It becomes a status report, it runs to 30 minutes, and nobody listens to anyone else.

**How to fix it:**

- **Walk the board, right to left.** Start with what's closest to Done and ask what it needs to get there. This focuses the conversation on finishing, not starting.
- **Talk about work, not people.** "This ticket is stuck in review" is more useful than "Yesterday I worked on the ticket".
- **Take problems offline.** If a discussion involves two people, they agree to talk straight after. Everyone else goes back to work.
- **Keep it to 15 minutes, hard.** If it regularly runs over, the team is too big or the board is too full.

## Sprint planning

**Output:** a sprint goal and a set of work the team believes it can finish.

**Common failure:** planning starts with unrefined tickets, so the team spends two hours working out what each one means. Or the product owner arrives with a list and the team just accepts it.

**How to fix it:**

- **Only bring refined work.** If a ticket hasn't been through refinement, it doesn't go into planning. No exceptions.
- **Start with the goal.** One sentence that describes what this sprint achieves. "Players can verify their identity without leaving the deposit flow" is a goal. "Do tickets 412 to 431" isn't.
- **Plan from capacity, not hope.** Account for holidays, on-call and the share of time unplanned work usually takes. If 20% of each sprint goes to incidents, plan for 80%.
- **Cap it.** For a two-week sprint, 90 minutes is plenty if refinement has done its job.

## Backlog refinement

**Output:** the next one to two sprints' worth of work, understood well enough to be planned and estimated.

**Common failure:** refinement becomes a long, unfocused meeting where the whole team reads tickets for the first time together. Or it's skipped entirely, and planning absorbs the cost.

**How to fix it:**

- **Prepare before the session.** The product owner shares the candidates in advance with acceptance criteria drafted. Engineers read them and come with questions.
- **Bring the right people for each item.** A backend-heavy ticket doesn't need the whole frontend team. Rotate attendance based on what's on the agenda.
- **Use a definition of ready.** A short checklist, for example: clear user value, acceptance criteria, designs attached where needed, dependencies identified, small enough for a sprint.
- **Split aggressively.** If something can't be finished in a few days, find a smaller slice that still delivers value.
- **Run it little and often.** Two 45-minute sessions a sprint usually work better than one long one.

## Sprint review

**Output:** feedback on what was built, and any change of direction that results.

**Common failure:** a slide deck, or a demo to an empty room. Stakeholders don't attend because nothing they say changes anything.

**How to fix it:**

- **Show working software.** Not screenshots, not tickets. The real thing, ideally in a staging environment that looks like production.
- **Invite the people who asked for it.** If marketing requested the feature, marketing should be there to say whether it does what they needed.
- **Ask questions, not for applause.** "Is this what you expected?" and "What would you change?" are better than "Any questions?"
- **Update the backlog live.** When feedback leads to a change, capture it during the meeting. People keep attending reviews when they see their input affect the plan.

## Retrospective

**Output:** one or two improvements the team commits to trying next sprint.

**Common failure:** the same complaints every two weeks, a long list of actions nobody follows up on, or a session that avoids anything uncomfortable.

**How to fix it:**

- **Start by checking last time's action.** Did we do it? Did it help? This alone changes how seriously people take retros.
- **Use data where you can.** Cycle time, carry-over, number of unplanned items. Feelings matter, but numbers ground the conversation.
- **Vary the format.** The same three columns every time gets stale. Try timelines, "start/stop/continue", or focusing on a single theme.
- **Commit to one thing.** One owned, specific improvement beats five vague ones.

## A healthy meeting budget

For a two-week sprint, a reasonable total is:

| Meeting | Frequency | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Daily standup | Daily | 15 min |
| Refinement | Twice per sprint | 45 min each |
| Sprint planning | Once | 90 min max |
| Sprint review | Once | 45–60 min |
| Retrospective | Once | 60 min |

That's roughly six hours per person per sprint, or about 7% of working time. If your team spends much more than that, look for meetings without a clear output. If it spends much less, check whether refinement or retros are being skipped, and whether planning is quietly paying the price.

## The real test

A good meeting leaves people knowing something they didn't know, or with a decision that wasn't there before. If your team walks out of most meetings with neither, cancelling a few won't fix it. Giving each one a job will.
